Step 1 — Live Demo: Show a Real Phishing Email
🖥️ DO THIS on screen first

Open your demo email inbox and find a phishing/spam email (most inboxes have them in the Spam folder). If none exists, use the mock examples below. Walk through it live using the "SLAM" framework:

  • Sender — who actually sent this?
  • Links — where do they actually go?
  • Attachments — is there anything unexpected?
  • Message — does the content feel right?
Real-World Example 1 — "Your Netflix Account is Suspended"
From:billing-alert@netflix-payments.support
To:you@gmail.com
Subject:⚠️ Your account has been suspended — action required
Date:Today, 11:42 PM

Dear Valued Customer,

We were unable to process your most recent payment. Your account has been temporarily suspended. To restore access, please update your billing information immediately.

⏰ You have 24 hours before your account is permanently deleted.

→ Click here to update your payment details

© Netflix Inc. | Unsubscribe | Privacy Policy

🚩 Red Flags to Point Out
  • Domain: netflix-payments.support — NOT netflix.com. Real domain is after the last dot before the path: netflix-payments.support
  • Urgency: "24 hours before permanent deletion" — classic fear trigger
  • Generic salutation: "Dear Valued Customer" — Netflix knows your name
  • Sent late at night: reduces critical thinking
  • Link text ≠ URL: hover the link — it goes to a different IP
💬 SAY THIS

"Notice the sender domain — netflix-payments.support. Attackers register cheap domains that sound official. The real Netflix will always email from @netflix.com. This is domain spoofing — and it works because people read the display name, not the actual address."

Real-World Example 2 — Microsoft 365 Password Expiry (Most Common in Offices)
From:IT-Security@microsoftonline-admin.com
To:employee@yourcompany.com
Subject:[Action Required] Your password expires in 1 day

Your Microsoft 365 password will expire in 1 day.

To continue using your Office 365 account without interruption, please reset your password immediately:

Keep Same Password →  Change Password →

Microsoft Corporation | One Microsoft Way | Redmond, WA

🚩 Why This Works So Well
  • Employees do receive real password-expiry emails — so this seems familiar
  • Domain: microsoftonline-admin.com — real is microsoft.com
  • Two CTA buttons create choice anxiety — both go to the same phishing page
  • "Keep Same Password" button is crafted to appeal to lazy users
  • Footer copies Microsoft's real address — completely meaningless in email
❓ ASK THE GROUP

"If you saw this email on a Monday morning, rushing before a meeting — what would you do? Hands up if you'd click 'Keep Same Password'?"

(This always generates honesty — use it as a teaching moment, not shame.)

🌍 Real Incident: FACC Aerospace (2016)

Austrian aerospace company FACC lost €50 million in a CEO fraud attack. A finance employee received a realistic-looking email from "the CEO" requesting a wire transfer for a confidential acquisition. No verification was done. CEO and CFO were both fired.

  • Domain: yourcompany-hq.com ≠ yourcompany.com
  • "Can't talk" prevents voice verification
  • "Do not discuss with others" — social isolation
  • Urgency + pressure: deal closes today
  • Never ask: would your CEO ever do this over email?

Step 1 — The "Real Domain" Rule (On Whiteboard / Screen)
🖥️ DO THIS: Write these on screen and walk through them

Rule: The real domain is always the word immediately before the last dot and TLD

URL (show on screen)Real DomainVerdict
https://www.paypal.com/signinpaypal.com✓ Legit
https://paypal.com.secure-login.net/accountsecure-login.net☠ Malicious
https://secure.paypa1.com/signinpaypa1.com (typosquat)☠ Malicious
https://signin.microsoft.com/authmicrosoft.com✓ Legit
https://microsoft.com.login-verify.xyz/authlogin-verify.xyz☠ Malicious
💬 SAY THIS

"Attackers abuse the subdomain system. You can put anything before a dot — so they put 'paypal.com' as a subdomain of their own malicious domain. Your eye sees 'paypal.com' and thinks it's safe. Always find the word immediately before .com / .net / .org / .in — that is the real owner of the website."

Real-World URL Attack Examples
🌍 The PayPal RockPhish Campaign (2006–2010)

One of the most prolific phishing operations in history used fast-flux DNS with URLs like paypa1.com and paypa-security.net. At peak, it harvested 50,000+ credentials/day. The campaign lasted 4 years because users couldn't spot the "1" vs "l" switch.

🌍 Google Docs Phishing (2017)

A mass phishing campaign used the real Google OAuth URL but tricked users into granting permissions to a malicious app called "Google Docs." The URL was legitimately google.com — but the OAuth permission screen was crafted by attackers. Over 1 million Gmail accounts compromised in under an hour.

💡 Lesson: A legit URL alone is not enough — check what you're authorising.

🌍 COVID-19 WHO URL Scam (2020)

Attackers registered hundreds of domains like who-covid19-update.com, worldhealthorg.info. Emails claimed to be from WHO with "urgent pandemic safety measures." URL shorteners (bit.ly) were also used to hide the destination. WHO's real domain is who.int.

🌍 SolarWinds Supply Chain (2020)

The attackers used the domain avsvmcloud.com as a C2 server — completely unrecognisable as malicious by name. 18,000 organisations including US government agencies were affected. This shows attackers also use completely novel domains — not just typosquats.

HTTPS Misconception — "The Lock = Safe" Myth
🔓 Busting the Most Dangerous Myth in Cybersecurity

Over 83% of phishing sites now use HTTPS (with a padlock). The padlock only means the connection is encrypted — it says nothing about whether the website is legitimate.

The padlock means: "Your data is securely transmitted to the thief."

💬 SAY THIS (with analogy)

"Imagine a sealed envelope. HTTPS means the envelope is sealed — nobody can peek inside while it travels. But if the address on the envelope leads to a criminal's mailbox, sealing it just makes the criminal's job easier. The padlock tells you nothing about who is receiving your data."

Real Password Breach Examples to Show
🌍 RockYou Breach (2009) — Worst Password List Ever

32 million plaintext passwords exposed. The top 5 most used:

  1. 123456
  2. 12345
  3. 123456789
  4. password
  5. iloveyou

Today the RockYou2021 dataset contains 8.4 billion breached passwords — used in every major brute-force attack. If your password is on this list, it will be cracked in under 1 second.

🌍 LinkedIn Breach (2012 → 2016)

117 million hashed passwords stolen in 2012 but not cracked until 2016 — then sold on the dark web. Many users had reused these passwords on other sites, leading to a wave of account takeovers at Dropbox, GitHub, and other platforms.

💡 Password reuse is the #1 cause of account takeovers. One breach becomes many.

🌍 SolarWinds — The Password in GitHub (2020)

Before the major supply chain attack, a researcher found SolarWinds had published its update server password solarwinds123 in a public GitHub repository. An intern had uploaded it by mistake. This single password gave initial access to a system used by 18,000 organisations including multiple US government agencies.

🌍 Colonial Pipeline Ransomware (2021)

The attackers used a single compromised VPN password (no MFA) found in a previous data breach. They didn't hack any software — they just logged in. The attack shut down fuel supply to the US East Coast for 6 days. The company paid $4.4 million in ransom.

💡 If Colonial Pipeline had MFA on their VPN, this attack would have been stopped.

MFA — Why It Stops 99.9% of Attacks
🔐 Microsoft Research Finding

Microsoft's own data shows that enabling MFA blocks 99.9% of automated account compromise attacks. Even if an attacker has your exact password, without the second factor they cannot get in.

The Colonial Pipeline attack was stopped — only in hindsight — because: VPN had no MFA. If MFA had been enabled, the breach would not have occurred.

Step 1 — Real Incident: Twitter Hack 2020
🌍 The Twitter/X Bitcoin Scam Hack — July 15, 2020

What happened: Attackers (a 17-year-old and accomplices) called Twitter employees pretending to be IT support. They socially-engineered staff into providing access credentials to internal admin tools. They then hijacked 130 high-profile accounts including:

Barack ObamaJoe BidenElon MuskBill GatesAppleJeff Bezos

The hijacked accounts all posted: "I'm giving back to the community due to COVID-19. Send Bitcoin and I'll double it."

Result: $120,000 in Bitcoin stolen in hours. 3 arrested. Twitter's stock dropped 4% the next day.

🚩 The Attack Chain
Phone call to Twitter employees
Pretending to be internal IT, asked employees to go to a fake VPN login page
Credentials harvested in real-time
Attackers entered them immediately into the real Twitter internal system
MFA bypass via SIM swap
For employees with MFA, attackers simultaneously SIM-swapped their phones to intercept the OTP
Admin access to high-profile accounts
Used "agent tools" to take over verified accounts and post the Bitcoin scam
💬 SAY THIS

"The Twitter hack didn't use any zero-day exploits. No sophisticated malware. The attackers simply called people on the phone and asked nicely — pretending to be IT. This is social engineering at scale. And it worked on employees of one of the world's most security-conscious companies. What would you do if you got that call?"

Real Incident 2 — Colonial Pipeline (Know the Full Story)
🌍 Colonial Pipeline Ransomware Attack — May 2021
Day 0 — Credential breach found on dark web
Attackers found a VPN username and password from a previous data breach. The employee had reused their password.
Day 0 — Logged into VPN (no MFA)
Single factor VPN. No second verification. Attackers gained access to the corporate network.
Day 1–5 — DarkSide ransomware deployed
100GB data exfiltrated before encryption. All operational systems encrypted.

  2. Dark web monitoring for leaked credentials
  3. Password manager so the employee wouldn't reuse passwords
  4. Network segmentation so OT (operational tech) was isolated from IT
  5. Regular security awareness training

5 Real-World Attacks — Use These During Your Training
1. The Podesta Email Hack — 2016 US Election
Attack Type: Spear Phishing · Target: Hillary Clinton Campaign · Attacker: APT28 (Russian state)
Impact: US Election disruption

What happened: John Podesta, Clinton's campaign chairman, received an email claiming to be from Google: "Someone has your password. Change it immediately." The email linked to a fake Google login page. His aide checked with IT and was mistakenly told it was "legitimate." Podesta entered his credentials. 50,000+ emails stolen and published on WikiLeaks.

The IT person meant to type "This is an ILLEGITIMATE email" but typed "legitimate" by accident — one word changed the course of history.

Lessons for Training:

  • Even senior, educated targets fall for phishing
  • Verbal confirmations can go wrong — always use written, official channels
  • Google never emails you about password compromises via links — it alerts you in-app
  • Always navigate to sensitive sites directly, never via email links
2. The WannaCry Ransomware — NHS UK (2017)
Attack Type: Ransomware via phishing + unpatched systems · Target: NHS Hospitals across UK
Impact: £92M + 19,000 appointments cancelled

What happened: WannaCry ransomware spread via a phishing email and an unpatched Windows vulnerability (EternalBlue). NHS hospitals had systems running Windows XP — no longer receiving security updates. Once in, the ransomware spread laterally, encrypting CT scanners, MRI machines, and patient records.

Surgeries were cancelled. Ambulances were diverted. Patients in critical condition were turned away. All for the equivalent of ~$300 in Bitcoin ransom per machine.

Lessons for Training:

  • Phishing is the initial entry point — but unpatched systems amplify damage
  • Ransomware doesn't just encrypt data — it halts critical operations
  • Cost of prevention (patches, training) << cost of breach
  • One infected machine on a network can spread to thousands instantly
3. The Ubiquiti Employee Data Theft — 2021
Attack Type: Insider threat via spear phishing · Target: Networking hardware company Ubiquiti
Impact: $4.1B market cap loss

What happened: An employee at Ubiquiti received a targeted spear-phishing email. After clicking, their credentials were used to access Ubiquiti's AWS cloud infrastructure. The attacker then posed as an anonymous security researcher, demanding $2M in ransom to not publish the stolen data.

It later emerged the "attacker" was actually a current Ubiquiti employee trying to profit from access they already had — an insider threat disguised as external attack.

Lessons for Training:

  • Cloud access = high-value target. Protect with MFA always
  • Insider threats are real — not just external hackers
  • Principle of least privilege: employees should only access what they need
  • All suspicious access (internal or external) must be reported
4. Indian Bank BEC Fraud — Multiple Cases (2022–2024)
Attack Type: Business Email Compromise (CEO Fraud) · Target: SMEs and finance teams across India
Impact: ₹1,000+ crore stolen annually

What happened: Attackers compromised or spoofed email accounts of company CEOs/CFOs and sent urgent wire transfer instructions to finance teams. In multiple documented cases, employees transferred funds to overseas accounts before realising the email was fraudulent. One Pune-based IT company lost ₹7.2 crore in a single transaction.

The RBI issued multiple advisories about BEC fraud — yet cases continue to rise because the emails are incredibly convincing and exploit trust in authority.

Lessons for Training:

  • Always call back on a known number to verify wire transfer requests
  • No legitimate CEO bypasses finance approval chains "urgently"
  • Check the actual "From" address, not just the display name
  • Implement dual-approval for transfers above a threshold (policy fix)
5. The 2023 MGM Resorts Hack — $100M in Losses
Attack Type: Vishing (Voice Phishing) + Social Engineering · Target: MGM Resorts International
Impact: $100M revenue loss + massive reputational damage

What happened: The ALPHV/Scattered Spider hacking group spent 10 minutes on a phone call with MGM's IT helpdesk. They found an MGM employee's name on LinkedIn, called IT pretending to be that employee, and reset their credentials. This gave them access to Okta SSO — which cascaded into control of MGM's entire environment.

Slot machines went dark. Hotel key cards stopped working. Casino floor systems froze. 10 minutes on the phone = 10 days of disruption and $100M loss.

Lessons for Training:

  • Helpdesk staff need strict identity verification protocols before any reset
  • SSO is powerful — but one compromised identity = everything compromised
  • LinkedIn profiles are reconnaissance goldmines for attackers
  • The weakest link is always human, not technical
  • Training the helpdesk is as important as training users
Common Participant Questions & How to Answer Them
"I use Gmail / Outlook — aren't they already filtering all phishing emails?"

Good filters catch a lot — but not everything. Google and Microsoft report that even their best filters miss 3–7% of phishing emails. With billions of emails per day, that's hundreds of millions that reach inboxes. Additionally, attackers constantly evolve their techniques to evade filters. A filter is your second line of defence. Your judgement is the first.

"I've seen these emails for years — I'd never fall for it."

This is the most dangerous belief in cybersecurity. The MGM Resorts, Twitter, Podesta, and Colonial Pipeline attacks all targeted experienced professionals. Attackers specifically target people who are busy, distracted, or under pressure. The question isn't "Am I smart enough?" — it's "Am I busy enough to make a mistake?" The answer is always yes.

Also: modern spear-phishing emails are personalised using your LinkedIn, social media, and company website. They reference your name, your manager's name, recent company events. They don't look like spam anymore.

"What if I accidentally clicked a phishing link?"
  • Do NOT enter any information on the page that opened
  • Close the browser tab immediately
  • Disconnect from Wi-Fi or ethernet temporarily
  • Report it to IT security immediately — even if nothing seems wrong. Fast reporting can prevent malware from spreading across the network
  • Change your password for any accounts you use on that device
  • IT will scan your device for malware

Key message: Reporting protects everyone. IT teams are not there to punish — they need to know ASAP.

"My password is already long with symbols — isn't that enough?"

Length and uniqueness matter more than complexity. A password like Ahmed@Mumbai2024! is 18 characters with symbols but it follows predictable human patterns (name, city, year, symbol at end). Attackers use rule-based cracking that knows these patterns.

The real question is: are you reusing it? If yes, one breach of any site where you use it = all your accounts compromised. Use a password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password) to generate and store unique passwords for every site.

"Is a VPN enough to protect me from phishing?"

No. A VPN encrypts your connection and hides your IP address — it does nothing to stop you from entering your credentials on a phishing site. In fact, using a VPN can give a false sense of security. Phishing is about deceiving you, not attacking your connection. The same vigilance applies whether you're on a VPN, public Wi-Fi, or office network.

"My company uses SSO — doesn't that mean I only need one password?"

SSO is convenient but creates a single point of failure. If your SSO credentials are phished, the attacker gets access to everything your SSO controls — email, files, HR systems, cloud resources. This is exactly what happened to MGM Resorts ($100M loss). SSO should always be protected by MFA. One SSO credential + no MFA = catastrophic exposure.

"What's the difference between antivirus and what we're learning today?"

Antivirus detects and blocks known malicious files and programs. It cannot stop you from voluntarily entering your credentials on a fake website. It cannot stop you from wiring money to a fraudster. It cannot stop you from clicking a link to a brand-new phishing page that was registered yesterday (no antivirus has analysed it yet). Human awareness is the security layer that covers what no software can — intentional human actions.

"How do attackers even find my email address to target me?"
  • Data breaches: Your email was in one of the many breaches (check HaveIBeenPwned)
  • LinkedIn and company websites: Most professionals list emails publicly
  • Email harvesting bots: Scan websites automatically for @domain patterns
  • Purchased lists: Breached email databases are sold on dark web for a few dollars
  • Social media: Many people use the same email for everything

Assume attackers already have your work email. The question is whether they can trick you once they send something to it.
